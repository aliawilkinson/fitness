# Eat, Sleep, Move, Make It Fit

## Practical fitness foundations course proposal

**Status:** Curriculum proposal for customer validation. This is a course map, not a completed course or an individualized medical or nutrition plan.

## The job of the course

Help a woman who wants to take better care of herself but feels unsure, bored, overwhelmed, or inconsistent answer four questions:

1. What matters for my health and goals?
2. Why does each part matter?
3. What should I do in ordinary life?
4. How do I make it fit into an actual Tuesday?

The course should leave her able to choose an activity, plan the whole outing, perform a basic workout safely, assemble ordinary meals, protect sleep, understand common tradeoffs, and know when she needs a trainer, dietitian, or clinician.

It can be sold as a standalone foundations course and included with recurring coaching. The supported version adds assessment, feedback, accountability, and personalization; the course gives every client a shared vocabulary before paid coaching time is used.

## The product promise

**“Learn how to care for your body and make fitness work in real life—even if you do not love the gym.”**

This is broader and more useful than a library of exercise videos. It joins health literacy with execution: choosing, scheduling, packing, traveling, starting, adjusting, and recovering.

## First experience: a foundations consult

Begin with a 60–75 minute consult rather than asking a beginner to complete a long intake alone. The consult should produce one useful week, not an idealized life overhaul.

### Before the call

Collect only information that changes the recommendation:

- goals and why they matter now;
- current movement, meals, sleep, alcohol, and cannabis patterns;
- activities she enjoys, dislikes, or will only do socially;
- schedule, commute, facilities, budget, equipment, and available companions;
- injuries, pain, health conditions, medications, pregnancy considerations, relevant family history, and prior eating-disorder concerns;
- what has made previous attempts easy or difficult.

### During the call

1. Explain the four pillars and identify the current bottleneck.
2. Choose one enjoyable or social movement commitment and one small supporting action.
3. Build the first activity door to door, including travel and getting ready afterward.
4. Agree on a minimum version for a difficult day.
5. Identify questions that require a trainer, registered dietitian, or clinician.

### Takeaway

Give the participant a one-page plan containing her calendar block, packing list, location, companion or class, meal and hydration plan, sleep opportunity, fallback, and next check-in.

## Course architecture

Each lesson follows the same format:

1. **Why it matters:** a short explanation without scare tactics.
2. **What good enough looks like:** the practical target or decision rule.
3. **How to do it:** a demonstration, template, or worked example.
4. **Make it fit:** time, money, location, social, and energy constraints.
5. **Try it once:** one observable action.
6. **Get help when:** the boundary between general education and individual care.

Most teaching segments should take 5–12 minutes. The exercise or planning task is the lesson; watching content is preparation for it.

## Module 0 — Know your starting point

**Purpose:** Replace vague pressure to “get healthy” with a usable starting point.

Lessons:

- What fitness includes: health, capacity, skill, appearance, enjoyment, confidence, and participation.
- Goals versus methods: looking different may be a goal; lifting, dancing, climbing, walking, and meal planning are methods.
- Baselines that help without becoming a judgment: current activity, energy, sleep, pain, schedule, confidence, and one or two performance measures.
- Measurements and their limits: scale weight, circumference, photos, body-fat estimates, strength, endurance, mobility, and how measurement error affects interpretation.
- What personal training is for: assessment, technique coaching, suitable progression, program decisions, accountability, problem solving, and adapting around constraints.
- Who does what: trainer, registered dietitian, physician, physical therapist, and mental-health professional.

**Practical task:** Complete the foundations consult and choose the first seven-day experiment.

## Module 1 — Eat

**Purpose:** Teach a repeatable way to feed a body without making every meal a math problem.

Lessons:

- Energy balance and body-weight change, including why estimates are starting points rather than promises.
- Protein, carbohydrate, fat, fiber, and micronutrients: what each does and where ordinary foods supply them.
- A flexible meal-building method: protein source, produce, carbohydrate, fat or flavor, and enough food for the context.
- Creating a meal rhythm when there is no normal eating schedule.
- Food before and after activity: comfort, timing, recovery, and easy options.
- Reading a label, estimating a portion, eating out, and handling social meals.
- Macros: when grams or percentages help, how an estimate is made, and why there is no universal “best split.”
- Hydration: teach the National Academies' adult-woman reference of about 2.7 liters (91 fluid ounces) of **total** water per day as a population starting point, not an exact personal prescription. Total water includes food and every beverage; survey data underlying the reference attributed about 2.2 liters (74 fluid ounces) to beverages. Then adjust the working plan for heat, activity, illness, pregnancy, and clinical restrictions. Do not teach a body-weight formula as an exact requirement or tell everyone to add 91 ounces of plain water on top of food and other drinks.
- Bone-supporting nutrition and the roles of calcium, vitamin D, protein, and adequate energy.
- Supplements, deficiency claims, and lab results: what can be taught generally and what needs credential-appropriate assessment or clinical confirmation.

**Practical tasks:** Build three meals the participant would really eat; create a grocery fallback list; plan food and drink around one activity outing.

## Module 2 — Sleep and recover

**Purpose:** Make recovery something the participant can schedule and observe.

Lessons:

- Sleep quantity, quality, and regularity. Adults ages 18–60 are generally advised to get at least seven hours, but the plan should allow enough time in bed for the person to obtain the sleep she needs.
- Planning backward from wake time: bedtime routine, wind-down time, and realistic morning preparation.
- Light, caffeine, meals, alcohol, cannabis, temperature, noise, and screens as variables to test.
- Training stress and recovery: soreness, fatigue, rest days, lighter days, and why more is not automatically better.
- Stress, menstrual-cycle observations, illness, travel, and life changes as planning inputs rather than moral failures.
- When persistent insomnia, heavy snoring, extreme daytime sleepiness, pain, or unusual fatigue needs clinical attention.

**Practical tasks:** Run a seven-day sleep-opportunity experiment and record bedtime, wake time, interruptions, and how rested the participant feels.

## Module 3 — Move

**Purpose:** Explain the different jobs movement can do and make the first workouts safe and understandable.

Lessons:

- The movement menu:
  - aerobic work improves cardiorespiratory capacity and can come from walking, dancing, cycling, sports, classes, and many other activities;
  - strength work helps build or retain muscle, supports bones, and makes daily and athletic tasks easier;
  - mobility and flexibility support usable range of motion;
  - balance, coordination, power, and skill matter in different activities and stages of life.
- Public-health baseline: work toward at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, or 75 minutes vigorous, each week plus muscle strengthening on two or more days. Beginners can start with smaller amounts and build.
- Choosing for adherence: enjoyment, friends, learning, location, price, and schedule belong beside physical goals.
- Anatomy of a session: arrive and assess, warm up, practice the main activity, perform supporting work, cool down if useful, and record what happened.
- How a simple strength program works: movement patterns, exercises, sets, repetitions, rest, effort, load, frequency, recovery, and progression.
- How to combine a social or skill activity with the minimum useful supporting strength work.
- Form: stable setup, controlled range, breathing, feedback, and the difference between a useful cue and a universal rule. Teach form with live trainer observation or trainer-reviewed demonstrations.
- Gym orientation: entry, lockers, equipment adjustment, sharing equipment, cleaning, asking staff for help, spotting, and emergency procedures.
- Safety: distinguish ordinary effort and next-day soreness from sharp, sudden, worsening, or function-limiting pain; stop when symptoms make continued activity unsafe; escalate urgent symptoms appropriately.
- What to buy: start with activity-appropriate shoes and clothing, water access, any required safety equipment, and the venue's actual rules. Avoid a large beginner shopping list.

**Practical tasks:** Tour one venue; practice equipment setup; complete one social or skill session and one short trainer-approved strength session; write a repeatable workout card.

## Module 4 — Make It Fit

**Purpose:** Teach the invisible work that determines whether a plan happens.

Lessons:

- Calculate the full calendar block: prepare and pack, travel, park and walk, check in and change, arrival buffer, activity, shower and dress, travel to the next destination, and unpack.
- Plan backward from a class or appointment start time.
- Run a first-visit rehearsal: route, parking, entrance, locker room, check-in process, cancellation rules, and what to bring.
- Reduce friction: prepacked bag, duplicate toiletries, saved parking location, recurring calendar block, and a nearby or at-home option.
- Use the participant's real motivators: book with a friend, join a team or cohort, choose a skill progression, or make the trainer appointment the anchor.
- Design three versions of the plan: full, shorter, and minimum. Each version must be safe and intentionally useful.
- Use a three-visit time audit. Replace guesses with actual travel, parking, changing, and session times.
- Plan transitions: work to class, class to dinner, activity to shower, or home to the next destination.
- Handle missed plans with a reschedule rule and a diagnosis of the obstacle, not punishment.

**Practical task:** Complete the [Workout Outing Planner](workout-outing-planner.md), put the entire block on the calendar, and update estimates after the outing.

## Module 5 — Alcohol, cannabis, and real life

**Purpose:** Give adults accurate tradeoffs and practical harm-reduction tools without pretending either substance is required or risk-free.

Lessons:

- Alcohol: serving size versus a U.S. standard drink; effects on coordination, judgment, sleep, hydration, appetite, recovery, and longer-term health risk.
- Cannabis: product and THC variability; effects on attention, memory, reaction time, coordination, and judgment; delayed and unpredictable effects of edibles; respiratory exposure from smoking.
- Alcohol and cannabis together can cause greater impairment than either alone.
- Practical planning: decide before using, know what and how much is in the product, avoid rapid redosing, arrange sober transportation, do not drive or train while impaired, avoid mixing, protect sleep opportunity, and store cannabis securely away from children and pets.
- Review medications and health conditions with an appropriate clinician or pharmacist. Pregnancy, trying to conceive, and breastfeeding change the guidance and belong in a separate evidence-reviewed lesson.
- Notice patterns: frequency, amount, reason for use, effects on sleep or plans, and difficulty cutting back. Include a nonjudgmental route to professional help.

**Practical task:** Complete one private “planned versus actual” log covering amount, timing, food, transportation, sleep, next-day effects, and whether the choice supported the participant's priorities.

## Module 6 — Keep, change, or get help

**Purpose:** Teach adjustment without dependence on the course.

Lessons:

- Review behavior and outcomes over two to four weeks rather than reacting to one day.
- Change one important variable at a time when practical.
- Progress activity gradually and protect enjoyment and social connection.
- Know what requires individualized coaching: technique, program progression, exercise substitutions, accountability, and confidence in a new setting.
- Know what requires clinical or regulated nutrition care: symptoms, injuries, eating disorders, medication interactions, lab interpretation, deficiencies, pregnancy-related care, or therapeutic diets.

**Graduation task:** Keep what worked, revise one bottleneck, and choose whether to continue independently, join recurring programming, or purchase individual coaching.

## Four-week supported pilot

The full curriculum is larger than the first experiment. Test a thin, useful version:

| Week | Live focus | Independent action | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Consult + Make It Fit | Plan and complete one full outing | Did the outing happen? Where did time differ? |
| 2 | Move | One enjoyable/social session + one short supporting session | Confidence, enjoyment, questions, trainer time |
| 3 | Eat + hydration | Build a repeatable day and fuel one outing | Feasibility, hunger/energy observations, support needed |
| 4 | Sleep + real-life choices | Run one recovery experiment and make the next-month plan | Completion, outcome, willingness to continue or pay |

Do not require the participant to love lifting. The product should help her understand strength training, experience a sensible version, and decide how it can support activities she values.

## Trainer, course, and future software boundaries

The trainer should author and approve the exercise demonstrations, coaching standards, and credential-appropriate nutrition content. The collaborators should separately agree who owns or may reuse the finished materials. A qualified reviewer should check health, substance, pregnancy, and clinical-nutrition lessons before publication.

Useful software can later:

- calculate and calendar the door-to-door activity block;
- generate packing and transition checklists;
- help compare activities by enjoyment, social support, time, price, and physical purpose;
- preserve trainer-approved workout cards and explanations;
- log actual times, questions, and confidence;
- suggest the agreed full, short, or minimum version when plans change.

Do not position the first software version as a diagnosis tool, deficiency detector, autonomous medical or nutrition prescriber, or reliable substitute for live form assessment. Its strongest initial job is turning an approved plan into an outing the participant can complete.

## Product validation questions

Measure whether the course resolves an expensive enough problem:

- Did participants complete more planned activity outings?
- Did uncertainty about the gym, workout, food, or schedule fall?
- Which lesson changed behavior rather than only increasing knowledge?
- What still required the trainer?
- Did participants use the full-time planner and correct its estimates?
- Would a buyer pay for the course alone, the supported version, recurring programming, or personal training?
- Does inclusion of the course improve coaching onboarding or retention?
