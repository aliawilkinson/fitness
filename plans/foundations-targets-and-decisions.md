# Fitness Foundations: Targets and Decision Guide

**Status:** Research-informed course content and starting framework. It is not an individualized prescription. Exact targets require the participant's measurements, schedule, health context, preferences, and professional scope.

## What is important

The course should teach six connected jobs:

1. **Eat enough of the right things:** energy, protein, fiber, micronutrients, and a meal pattern the participant can repeat.
2. **Sleep and recover:** enough sleep opportunity, manageable training stress, and attention to persistent fatigue or pain.
3. **Move in several ways:** aerobic activity, strength, mobility or flexibility, balance, and enjoyable skill or social movement.
4. **Make the plan executable:** calendar time, travel, parking, clothing, food, showering, money, social support, and fallback plans.
5. **Understand real-life tradeoffs:** alcohol, cannabis, stress, illness, travel, and reproductive considerations.
6. **Measure and adjust:** use trends to change the smallest relevant part of the plan and know when professional help is appropriate.

The participant does not need to optimize all six at once. Determine the largest current bottleneck, make one week work, and then add complexity.

## The decision sequence

For every target, teach the same sequence:

> **Inputs → estimate → calendar or action → observe → adjust**

An estimate is successful when it produces a useful action and can be corrected from experience. It does not have to predict the body perfectly.

## 1. Establish safety and context

### Inputs

- age, height, weight, and pregnancy or breastfeeding status;
- injuries, pain, dizziness, fainting, chest symptoms, unusual shortness of breath, and exercise restrictions;
- medical conditions, medications, and clinician instructions;
- menstrual-cycle changes, prior eating-disorder concerns, and relevant family history;
- current activity and experience with equipment;
- alcohol, cannabis, nicotine, and other substance use that affects safety or recovery.

### Starting rule

General education can proceed while questions outside a trainer's scope are sent to the appropriate clinician, physical therapist, pharmacist, or registered dietitian. New or unexplained symptoms should not be solved by changing a workout or supplement on guesswork.

For a participant with a family history of osteoporosis, include weight-bearing activity, progressive resistance training, adequate energy and protein, and calcium and vitamin D education. Family history raises risk but does not by itself diagnose osteoporosis.

### Observe and adjust

Record pain or symptoms, relevant professional guidance, and exercise modifications. Update the plan when the health context changes.

## 2. Determine what can fit

### Inputs

- fixed work, care, sleep, and social commitments;
- facility hours and class times;
- preparation, travel, parking, check-in, changing, showering, and onward travel;
- preferred companions and activities;
- realistic weekly money and energy budgets.

### Calculation

For each candidate activity:

> **Total outing time = preparation + outbound travel + parking/walking + check-in/changing + arrival buffer + activity + shower/dressing + onward travel + arrival/unpacking**

Use the [Workout Outing Planner](workout-outing-planner.md). Put total outing time on the calendar. After three visits, replace estimates with the actual median time for each segment.

### Starting rule

Book the activity that has the best combination of enjoyment, social commitment, useful movement, acceptable cost, and manageable total outing time. A theoretically perfect activity that is rarely attended is a poor starting choice.

### Observe and adjust

Track planned outings, completed outings, total time, enjoyment, and the point where the plan broke down. Change the bottleneck: venue, class time, companion, bag, transition, session length, or fallback.

## 3. Determine the movement plan

### Destination for general health

The public-health destination for adults is at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, 75 minutes vigorous, or an equivalent mix each week, plus muscle strengthening for all major muscle groups on at least two days. Activity can be divided into smaller pieces, and some activity is better than none.

This is a destination, not a pass/fail opening week.

### Determine aerobic activity

**Inputs:** current weekly minutes, preferred activities, skill interests, available companions, and total outing time.

**Intensity:** use the talk test. During moderate work, the participant can talk but not sing. During vigorous work, only a few words are comfortable before a breath.

**Starting rule:** preserve enjoyable movement first. Begin with one or two scheduled social or skill sessions and short walks that fit naturally. Add time or a session after the existing plan is happening reliably and recovery is acceptable.

**Observe:** weekly minutes, attendance, talk-test intensity, enjoyment, energy, and recovery.

### Determine strength training

**Inputs:** training history, technique, pain or restrictions, available equipment, and the activities or appearance goals strength should support.

**Starting session:** use five or six trainer-approved movements covering most of the body:

1. squat or step-up;
2. hip hinge or hip thrust;
3. push;
4. pull;
5. carry or trunk stability;
6. an optional goal-specific movement, such as an additional glute exercise.

Begin with two working sets of roughly 8–12 controlled repetitions for each movement at an effort that leaves about three good repetitions available. Rest long enough to repeat sound technique. The first sessions should prioritize equipment setup and trainer feedback over fatigue.

**Progression rule:** when the participant reaches the top of the planned repetition range with stable technique and the intended effort for two sessions, add the smallest practical amount of resistance or a small number of repetitions. Change one variable at a time.

**Frequency:** build toward two nonconsecutive whole-body sessions each week. For someone returning after a long break, one coached session in week one is a sensible entry point.

**Observe:** exercises, load, repetitions, effort, technique cues, pain, soreness, and whether the session was repeated.

### Determine mobility and flexibility work

**Inputs:** the range of motion needed for chosen activities, movement limitations observed by a qualified trainer, and discomfort or injury history.

**Starting rule:** use 5–10 minutes two or three times each week on the few movements that matter. Warm the body first, move under control, and use mild tension rather than pain. Dynamic movement can prepare for activity; longer relaxed holds often fit better afterward or separately.

**Observe:** whether the target movement becomes easier and whether usable range improves. More flexibility is not automatically better, and persistent pain needs assessment.

### Determine balance and skill work

Balance, coordination, agility, and power can come from dance, racket sports, climbing, martial arts, field sports, and targeted drills. Choose these based on the participant's interests and the demands of her activity. They can also make movement socially rewarding and worth repeating.

## 4. Determine food and macro targets

### Determine energy intake

**Inputs:** age, sex, height, weight, current activity, target, timeline, and pregnancy or breastfeeding status.

Use the [NIH Body Weight Planner](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/weight-management/body-weight-planner) for an initial adult calorie estimate. It models changes in energy needs over time and should not be used during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Treat the output as a starting hypothesis:

1. Choose a gradual, realistic timeline.
2. Follow the plan consistently enough to evaluate it.
3. Compare two to four weeks of average body weight, hunger, energy, sleep, training, and menstrual-cycle observations.
4. If adherence is reasonably high and the trend is materially different from the plan, revise the estimate modestly with the trainer or dietitian.

Avoid making a large calorie cut to force a deadline. Faster loss can conflict with training quality, muscle retention, adequate nutrition, bone health, and adherence.

### Determine protein

For a healthy adult performing resistance training, a practical educational range is approximately **1.2–1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day**. A large meta-analysis found no further average resistance-training gain in fat-free mass beyond about 1.6 g/kg/day. This is a range, not a requirement for every person.

Calculation:

> **Body weight in kilograms = pounds ÷ 2.205**
>
> **Daily protein range = kilograms × 1.2 to kilograms × 1.6**

If a high body weight makes the result impractical, or kidney disease, pregnancy, therapeutic nutrition, or another condition applies, use an appropriately qualified professional to select the basis and target.

For people who do not want to track, distribute a useful protein source across three meals and review the actual foods before assigning grams.

### Determine fat and carbohydrate

Protein is set first. The adult acceptable distribution range for fat is 20–35% of calories; the participant can begin within that range according to food preference, satiety, and clinical context. Carbohydrate fills much of the remaining energy and is especially useful for active training.

Optional calculation:

> **Protein calories = protein grams × 4**
>
> **Fat grams = chosen fat percentage × daily calories ÷ 9**
>
> **Carbohydrate grams = (daily calories − protein calories − fat calories) ÷ 4**

The percentage is adjustable. Food quality, fiber, micronutrients, and a repeatable eating pattern still matter; a macro split alone does not define a good diet.

### Determine fiber and meal structure

For women ages 31–50, **25 grams of fiber per day** is a useful reference. Increase gradually and pair it with adequate fluids.

A simple meal template is:

- protein source;
- fruit or vegetable;
- carbohydrate appropriate to appetite and activity;
- fat or flavor source;
- enough total food for the plan.

Start with three predictable meal anchors if an irregular schedule is the main obstacle. The clock times can vary; the purpose is to prevent the whole day from becoming an improvised decision.

### Determine bone-supporting nutrition

For adults ages 19–50, the calcium reference is **1,000 mg per day** from food and supplements combined. The vitamin D reference for adults ages 19–70 is **600 IU (15 mcg) per day**. Review food sources first and discuss testing or supplements with an appropriate clinician, especially when family history, medications, absorption problems, or a prior deficiency matters.

## 5. Determine hydration

For adult women, the National Academies' population reference is approximately **2.7 liters (91 fluid ounces) of total water per day**. Total water includes food and every beverage. The survey data behind the reference attributed about **2.2 liters (74 fluid ounces)** to beverages.

### Starting rule

Use roughly 2.2 liters of fluids across the day as an educational starting reference, then adjust for the individual's food, thirst, weather, activity, illness, pregnancy, breastfeeding, and medical restrictions. A person under fluid restriction should follow clinical guidance.

### Optional sweat-rate estimate

For long, hot, or very sweaty sessions:

> **Sweat loss in liters ≈ pre-exercise weight in kg − post-exercise weight in kg + liters consumed − urine produced**

Divide by exercise hours to estimate liters per hour under those specific conditions. This varies by weather and activity and is not a command to replace every lost drop during exercise. A coach or sports dietitian can help with longer-event hydration and electrolytes.

### Observe and adjust

Notice thirst, urine pattern, heat, session duration, and large exercise-related weight changes. Do not force water to meet a generic formula, and do not treat urine color alone as a diagnosis.

## 6. Determine sleep opportunity

Adults ages 18–60 are generally advised to obtain at least seven hours of sleep. The participant needs more time in bed than the intended sleep duration when falling asleep and waking during the night take time.

Calculation:

> **Lights-out time = required wake time − intended sleep − expected awake time during the sleep window**

Example: a 7:00 a.m. wake time, eight intended hours of sleep, and an expected 30 minutes to fall asleep gives a lights-out time of 10:30 p.m. Add the wind-down routine before that.

### Starting rule

Choose a consistent wake time and protect an eight-hour sleep opportunity for seven nights when practical. Keep the room quiet, dark, relaxing, and cool; move caffeine earlier if it affects sleep; and avoid large meals and alcohol close to bedtime.

### Observe and adjust

Record lights-out time, estimated sleep, awakenings, wake time, rested feeling, caffeine, alcohol, cannabis, and training. Persistent sleep problems, heavy snoring, breathing pauses, or significant daytime sleepiness need clinical evaluation.

## 7. Determine alcohol and cannabis choices

The aim is informed choice and lower risk, not a claim that a particular amount is safe for everyone.

### Establish the baseline

For two ordinary weeks, privately record:

- product, amount, and timing;
- reason for using;
- whether alcohol and cannabis were combined;
- food and transportation plan;
- bedtime, awakenings, and next-day energy;
- whether a planned meal, workout, or responsibility changed.

One U.S. standard drink contains about 14 grams of alcohol: approximately 12 ounces of 5% beer, 5 ounces of 12% wine, or 1.5 ounces of 40% spirits. A large pour or strong beer may contain multiple standard drinks.

Cannabis effects vary with dose, THC concentration, experience, and route. Edibles may take up to two hours to produce effects, making early redosing a common route to overconsumption.

### Starting harm-reduction rules

- Decide the plan before becoming impaired and use less or less often when practical.
- Know the serving and potency; avoid rapid redosing.
- Do not combine alcohol and cannabis.
- Do not drive, operate equipment, or exercise while impaired; arrange sober transportation.
- Do not use alcohol as a sleep tool; it may shorten sleep onset while fragmenting later sleep.
- Store cannabis products securely away from children and pets.
- Ask a clinician or pharmacist about medication and health-condition interactions.
- Use pregnancy-, preconception-, and breastfeeding-specific guidance when relevant.

### Observe and adjust

Compare planned with actual use and connect the choice to sleep, appetite, mood, training, money, and responsibilities. Difficulty staying within one's own plan is useful information and a reason to seek nonjudgmental professional support.

## 8. Review the system

Use a small weekly dashboard:

| Measure | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Planned and completed outings | Is the routine executable? |
| Aerobic minutes and strength sessions | Is movement approaching the intended mix? |
| Sleep opportunity and rested feeling | Is recovery sufficient? |
| Meal anchors and protein/fiber pattern | Is eating repeatable and adequate? |
| Alcohol/cannabis planned versus actual | Are choices supporting priorities? |
| Enjoyment and social connection | Is the plan worth repeating? |
| Pain, fatigue, hunger, and menstrual changes | Does the plan need adjustment or professional review? |
| Optional average weight and monthly circumference | Is the body trend consistent with the longer-term goal? |

Review every one or two weeks for behavior and every two to four weeks for body trends. Change the variable most likely to explain the result. If attendance is low, fix the outing. If technique is uncertain, use trainer feedback. If energy, hunger, sleep, menstrual function, or performance deteriorates, review total training and food rather than pushing harder.

## Four-week starting plan for a socially motivated returner

This is the course's default test, assuming no restriction or symptom changes it.

### Week 1 — Make one week real

- Complete the foundations consult.
- Book one enjoyable social or skill-based activity.
- Complete one trainer-led whole-body strength session.
- Take two 10–20 minute social walks or walking phone calls.
- Use three meal anchors, the fluid starting reference, and an eight-hour sleep opportunity.
- Log actual outing time, sleep, meals, and substance use without trying to perfect them.

### Week 2 — Repeat before adding

- Repeat the same social activity and strength session.
- Add a second short strength session only if recovery, technique, and schedule are acceptable.
- Build three repeatable meals and a backup grocery list.
- Correct the calendar blocks using actual travel and transition times.

### Week 3 — Build the useful mix

- Complete two social, skill, or aerobic sessions.
- Complete two nonconsecutive strength sessions.
- Continue short walks where they fit.
- Select protein and calorie estimates after the required measurements and health context are available.
- Run one alcohol- or cannabis-related experiment based on the baseline, such as using less often and observing sleep and next-day follow-through.

### Week 4 — Decide what deserves a place

- Repeat the week-three movement pattern.
- Review attendance, enjoyment, strength progression, recovery, food consistency, and actual time cost.
- Keep the activities that delivered enough benefit for their total outing time.
- Decide whether the next month needs independent repetition, recurring programming, or personal training.
