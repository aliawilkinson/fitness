# Founder fit and an Anaheim pilot

This note applies the [market assessment](../research/2026-09-13-us-market-gap/report.md) to the proposed collaboration. It is a business recommendation, not new evidence of demand. No offer has been launched and no one has been contacted.

**Commercial update:** the engineer has clarified a setup-and-support role. The current recommendation is the [sales-based services proposal](website-revenue-share-proposal.md), with the trainer retaining her business. The product and local-pilot reasoning below remains relevant.

## Context that changes the decision

The prospective collaborators are a personal trainer with nutrition expertise and a software engineer interested in learning to create products and earn income online. Both are based in Anaheim, California. The trainer's reported strengths include working with women and glute development, and her reported audience is approximately 14,000 followers. Audience geography, engagement, purchasing history, available working hours, and the collaborators' business arrangements remain unverified.

The strongest potential advantage is the combination of subject expertise, access to an existing audience, and the ability to improve delivery with software. Being near each other also makes joint observation, recording, and pilot delivery practical. Neither local residence nor follower count establishes demand.

The founders' ages do not justify restricting customers to the same age range. Choose a customer group based on an unresolved problem, access, actual purchases, and the trainer's ability to help.

## Recommended starting business

Test a **four-week supported strength-start program for women who want to start or return to strength training**. Include women who already walk, run, or take classes but want help adding strength. The outcome is an understandable routine they can carry out, with clearly limited access to a trainer when they get stuck.

This is a repeatable service with reusable educational material. It creates a way to test selling and delivery while learning which parts can become independent digital products. A foundations course remains useful: include it with the supported program, and test a separate self-paced purchase only when the material and its value are clear.

Do not assume that a successful four-week program needs an indefinite subscription. Continuing programming, group support, or individual coaching must provide a distinct ongoing benefit. A course that customers finish successfully can be a valid business.

This recommendation prioritizes obtaining customer and delivery evidence with a modest initial build. The preferred business model can change with the founders' income goals, time, and willingness to provide services.

| Intended business outcome | Most useful early emphasis |
|---|---|
| Repeatable side income | Sell a tightly scoped program, measure delivery time, and repeat it profitably |
| Replacing employment income | Add a realistic acquisition and capacity plan; count both people's work before projecting owner income |
| A scalable software company | Look for a frequent task that customers will pay software to solve; validate demand beyond this trainer and her audience |

The national market research does not prove that this particular program outranks mobility or weight-management offers. Those remain credible alternatives to compare if discovery or purchases point elsewhere.

## Suggested division of work

| Trainer | Engineer | Together |
|---|---|---|
| Select suitable participants; shape the routine and lessons; deliver and review exercise guidance | Build the smallest purchase and delivery flow; observe users; track support effort, purchases, refunds, and outcomes | Interview prospects, define the offer, sell it, review failures, and decide what to change |

The engineer's early product work should include customer conversations and selling. A functional application alone will not establish who buys, why they buy, or whether delivery is sustainable.

Before accepting money together, agree on responsibilities, weekly availability, ownership and permitted reuse of materials, compensation, expenses, and what happens if one person stops participating. This is a list of business decisions to resolve, not a proposed legal structure or an assumed agreement with the trainer.

## How Anaheim helps

Use local access to observe the real experience: reading a plan, finding equipment, dealing with a busy gym, and asking for help. Start through relationships the trainer already has, if she chooses to participate. Any hosted activity needs a suitable setting and the host's agreement; a public facility listing is not a partnership.

Keep delivery primarily remote if the intended business is online. A local observation session can improve the product, but special in-person assistance may make a pilot look more successful than the eventual remote service. Record that extra assistance and its cost.

For the first small paid cohort, label each participant's acquisition source, existing-client status, and local or remote delivery. Do not combine existing clients receiving an included course with new standalone purchasers in the sales denominator. Do not assume the trainer's followers live nearby.

After a locally informed pilot, repeat with remote participants who receive the intended standard service. This tests whether the offer works beyond proximity and personal relationships. Local success is useful evidence; it is not proof of national acquisition economics.

One relevant nearby support resource is the **Orange County Inland Empire SBDC**, which advertises no-cost entrepreneurial resources and confidential individual business consulting. It can be a place to review an offer, pricing assumptions, and basic business planning. Availability and the right advisor should be confirmed directly. [OCIE SBDC](https://ociesmallbusiness.org/) (checked September 13, 2026).

## The next practical milestone

Follow the existing [first-month pilot plan](first-month-gym-pilot.md): interview prospective buyers about recent obstacles, observe them using a credible existing solution, then offer a small paid cohort if a serviceable problem remains. The proposed milestone is approximately ten paying customers, explicitly a learning target rather than a validated demand threshold.

Write down the exact deliverable, support limits, price, dates, and refund terms before inviting payment. Treat the earlier plan's $49 price as its labeled low-support experiment, not a price recommendation for a more intensive service. Price the actual scope and count preparation, group sessions, individual help, administration, acquisition, and software costs.

Track collected revenue minus refunds, transaction charges, variable delivery costs, and acquisition expenses. Show both people's hours separately. Revenue is not profit, and unpaid founder time can conceal an unsustainable service.

Build custom software when repeated observations identify what to automate. Potential candidates include retrieving an approved explanation, maintaining one current plan, routing questions, and summarizing check-ins. Test whether each addition improves the experience or reduces delivery effort before treating it as a standalone product.

The immediate objective is to learn whether these collaborators can repeatedly find and serve paying customers. The longer-term options—course, supported membership, or software—should follow that evidence.
